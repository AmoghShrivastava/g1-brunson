A robot that dribbles between its legs.

G1 Brunson is a Unitree G1 humanoid taught, by trial and error inside a physics simulation, to do the between-the-legs crossover with a regulation basketball. No sensors on the ball. It sees it with its own head camera.

01 · Play

Can you out-dribble a robot?

Same rules the robot was trained on. Push the ball with the hand it is in, catch it with the other hand when it comes through. Twenty seconds. Brunson averages 44. Most humans with a keyboard get about 25.

Why it is hard: a crossover only counts if the bounce lands in the gap between the feet, the ball rose to at least 0.30 m first, and the other hand plays it next. Press early and the ball never gets high enough. Press late and it rolls away. The robot had 720 million practice bounces to find the timing.

03 · The reward

Be the referee.

Reinforcement learning is just: do something, get a score, do more of what scored. The whole trick was writing the scoring rule so that only a real crossover pays. Flip the switches to see how one bounce gets scored.

+0score for this bounce

Also in the rulebook
  • +1 every time a palm plays the ball
  • −0.3 for a bounce the robot did not cause
  • −5 if the ball rolls away and has to be replaced
  • −12 per metre the dribble apex is below 0.30 m
  • −50 for falling over (this one mattered most)
  • small charges for jerky motion, feet lifting, bad posture
04 · Perception

What the robot sees.

The G1 has a small depth camera in its head, tilted down. The robot renders what its own body should look like from its joint angles, subtracts that from the depth image, and whatever is left over and closer than its own body is the ball. Drag the slider.

head camera · colour
Why depth, not colour

Orange balls look grey in bad light and a court floor can be orange. Depth does not care about lighting, and the ball's size is known, so a sphere is fitted straight onto the pixels.

When the ball vanishes

Under the body the camera cannot see it. A tracker carries it through with a bounce model, the same way you know where the ball is when you look up.

The clever bit

That exact tracker, noise and dropouts included, was copied into the training simulation. The robot practised on what the camera would actually tell it, not on perfect information. Camera-in-the-loop: 41.8 crossovers vs 44.3 with perfect information.

08 · The name

Why "Brunson"?

Because the Knicks finally did it, and Jalen Brunson is the reason.

This project was built in the afterglow of the New York Knicks' championship run. Brunson is the guard who keeps the ball on a string, plays below the defender's hands, and never, ever looks rushed. That is exactly the behaviour we were trying to squeeze out of a 35-kilogram robot with rubber hands: low, tight, rhythmic, unbothered.

So the policy got a name instead of a version number. Every checkpoint before it is a letter of the alphabet. The one that finally dribbled like a point guard is G1 Brunson.

Also, "G1" is the robot model and Brunson wears #11. Two ones. We noticed after naming it and decided it was fate.

The uniform

During development the simulated robot wore a full Knicks kit, jersey and shorts, as separate garment meshes draped over the body. It was removed for the final renders so the judges could see the joints. It lives on in a folder called uniform_assets.
